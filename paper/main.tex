\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Background:} Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy targeting CD19 has revolutionized the treatment of refractory/relapsed B-cell lymphoma, yet response rates remain variable with only 40--60\% of patients achieving durable remission. T-cell receptor (TCR) repertoires of infused CAR-T products encode information about T-cell fitness and therapeutic potential, but traditional repertoire metrics have shown limited predictive value. Deep learning approaches offer potential to extract complex patterns from high-dimensional TCR data for clinical prediction.

\noindent\textbf{Methods:} We analyzed 239,634 eligible TCR beta-chain records from 34 patients with refractory/relapsed B-cell lymphoma treated with CD19 CAR-T therapy (18 responders, 16 non-responders). The supervised analysis used the DeepTCR sequence classifier with CDR3 amino acid sequences and V/J gene annotations. Five patient-stratified five-fold partitions crossed with two training seeds. Three- and four-fold sensitivity checks brought the total to 57 fits. A sequence-composition baseline used the same patient partitions. The exploratory unsupervised analysis used PCA and K-means clustering.

\noindent\textbf{Results:} The median within-fold patient AUROC across the ten five-fold evaluations was 0.456 (range 0.298--0.579), compared with 0.386 for the composition baseline across five partitions. DeepTCR scored higher than the matched baseline in eight of ten evaluations. AUROC was 0.438 in the three-fold and 0.611 in the four-fold sensitivity check. The exploratory analyses described repertoire composition and cluster occupancy within the cohort.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusions:} The patient-held-out results do not establish reliable response discrimination. The estimates varied across patient splits and training seeds. The repertoire summaries describe this cohort and provide hypotheses for independent validation.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} T-cell receptor, CAR-T, B-cell lymphoma, DeepTCR, cross-validation

\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{CD19 CAR-T Cell Therapy and Clinical Challenge}

Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy has revolutionized the treatment of hematological malignancies, particularly B-cell lymphomas \citep{june2018cart}. CD19-targeted CAR-T products (axicabtagene ciloleucel, tisagenlecleucel, lisocabtagene maraleucel) have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in refractory/relapsed diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), follicular lymphoma, and other B-cell neoplasms \citep{neelapu2017axicabtagene,schuster2019tisagenlecleucel}. These therapies redirect autologous T cells to recognize and eliminate CD19-expressing malignant B cells, achieving complete response rates of 40--60\% in heavily pretreated patients \citep{locke2019longterm}.

Despite these impressive outcomes, a substantial proportion of patients either fail to respond initially or relapse after initial response. Understanding the biological determinants of CAR-T therapy response remains a critical unmet need. Current predictive biomarkers include tumor burden, inflammatory markers (ferritin, CRP, LDH), and CAR-T product characteristics such as CD4/CD8 ratio and exhaustion markers \citep{locke2020tumor,deng2020characteristics}. However, these markers show variable predictive performance and fail to capture the complexity of T-cell repertoire composition within the infused product.

\subsection{CAR-T Product T-Cell Receptor Repertoires}

While CAR-T cells are engineered to express a synthetic chimeric receptor, they retain their endogenous T-cell receptor (TCR) repertoires \citep{fraietta2018determinants}. These native TCRs reflect the clonal history, differentiation state, and functional potential of the underlying T cells. A single-cell RNA and TCR sequencing study of large B-cell lymphoma reported that infusion products from responders were enriched for clonally expanded, highly activated CD8$^{+}$ T cells \citep{maurer2024baseline}. Importantly, TCR repertoire analysis provides a window into T-cell fitness that complements CAR expression-based metrics.

\subsection{T-Cell Receptor Biology and Repertoire Analysis}

T-cell receptors serve as the primary molecular interface between the adaptive immune system and tumor cells. Each TCR is composed of an alpha and beta chain, with the complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) of each chain forming the primary contact surface with peptide-major histocompatibility complex (pMHC) molecules \citep{davis1988t,rossjohn2015t}. The CDR3 region arises through V(D)J recombination, which generates extensive potential receptor diversity \citep{robins2009comprehensive}.

The TCR beta chain (TRB) is particularly informative for repertoire analysis due to its higher diversity compared to the alpha chain and its dominant role in determining peptide specificity \citep{glanville2017identifying}. TRB CDR3 sequences directly contact the peptide antigen, with specific amino acid motifs conferring recognition of distinct epitopes \citep{dash2017quantifiable}. V-gene and J-gene usage patterns further constrain peptide recognition, with certain V-J combinations enriched in tumor-reactive T-cell populations \citep{zhang2018investigation}.

High-throughput sequencing has enabled comprehensive profiling of TCR repertoires from peripheral blood and tumor tissue \citep{robins2009comprehensive,mamedov2013preparing}. Early studies established that increased TCR clonality and decreased diversity correlate with anti-PD-1 response in melanoma \citep{tumeh2014pd}. Subsequent work demonstrated that clonal expansion of tumor-infiltrating T cells predicts ipilimumab response \citep{postow2015peripheral}.

\subsection{Deep Learning for TCR Repertoire Classification}

Traditional TCR repertoire analysis relies on aggregate statistics such as Shannon entropy, Simpson diversity, and clonality indices \citep{greiff2015bioinformatic}. While informative, these metrics collapse high-dimensional sequence data into scalar values, potentially discarding predictive information. Machine learning approaches, particularly deep learning, offer the capability to learn complex, non-linear patterns directly from raw sequence data \citep{lecun2015deep}.

Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) provides an appropriate framework for repertoire classification \citep{dietterich1997solving}. In standard supervised learning, each sample has an associated label. However, TCR repertoires present a different challenge: each patient (label) is represented by thousands of sequences (instances), most of which are irrelevant to the classification task. MIL addresses this by treating the entire repertoire as a ``bag'' of sequences and learning to identify which sequences within each bag contribute to the patient-level prediction \citep{ilse2018attention}.

Attention mechanisms, originally developed for natural language processing \citep{vaswani2017attention}, have been adapted to weight instances within a repertoire \citep{ilse2018attention}. Sequence weights can help prioritize candidates for follow-up, but a weight alone cannot establish antigen reactivity.

DeepTCR, developed by Sidhom et al. \citep{sidhom2021deeptcr}, provides both sequence-level and repertoire-level classifiers. The supervised analysis reported here uses its sequence classifier and averages sequence scores within each patient.

\subsection{Study Objectives}

The primary objective of this study was to evaluate whether DeepTCR can discriminate CD19 CAR-T therapy response from pre-treatment TCR repertoires in refractory/relapsed B-cell lymphoma. We examined both supervised prediction and exploratory repertoire structure.

\textbf{Supervised Learning Objective:} Estimate discrimination on patients held out of model training, assess how much performance changes with different patient splits and training seeds, and compare with a sequence-composition baseline on the same splits.

\textbf{Unsupervised Learning Objective:} We aimed to discover natural clustering patterns in TCR repertoires that might reveal underlying biological structure associated with CAR-T product fitness, independent of response labels.

Secondary objectives were to compare the observed cluster distributions by response group and characterize CDR3 length, composition, and V/J gene usage in the cohort.

\section{Methods}

\subsection{Study Design and Patient Cohort}

This retrospective analysis of a public dataset used TCR repertoire data from 34 patients with refractory/relapsed B-cell lymphoma treated with CD19-targeted CAR-T cell therapy. The data are the single-cell TCR sequencing records of CAR-T infusion products that Li et al. \citep{li2023atlas} deposited in the Gene Expression Omnibus under accession GSE241783, a series covering 59 patients treated with axicabtagene ciloleucel, of whom 34 are analyzed here. Patients were classified as responders (n=18) or non-responders (n=16) using the archived response labels, described in the original submission as clinical response at day 90 post-infusion, with responders defined as achieving complete response (CR) or partial response (PR), and non-responders defined as stable disease (SD) or progressive disease (PD).

TCR sequencing was performed on the CAR-T cell products prior to infusion. The near-balanced cohort design (18 responders, 16 non-responders) helps prevent severe class imbalance from confounding model training. \subsection{TCR Sequencing and Data Processing}

The inputs were processed TRB repertoire records from the CAR-T products. Each record contained a CDR3 amino acid sequence, V and J annotations, and a patient identifier. The archived outcome labels were checked for consistency within patients.

The data processing pipeline is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}.
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Input checks:} The archived CDR3 sequences and V and J annotations were used as provided.
    \item \textbf{Length filtering:} Three records exceeded the classifier's 40-residue input limit. All 34 patients remained represented in the 239,634 eligible records.
    \item \textbf{Data standardization:} Column names were mapped to the fields used by DeepTCR.
    \item \textbf{Patient partitions:} Each repertoire and its response label were assigned together. No test patient contributed training records to that fold.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Feature Engineering and Encoding}

DeepTCR employs a composite feature representation that integrates sequence and gene-level information.

\textbf{Sequence Features:} The beta-chain CDR3 sequence was encoded with the package's amino acid mapping and padded to its 40-residue input width.

\textbf{Gene Features:} The recorded V and J gene annotations were passed to the model's categorical encoders. The vocabulary for each feature was fit on the training patients for that fold. Values absent from that vocabulary were assigned to the unknown category. Constant alpha-chain placeholders satisfied the package interface and supplied no patient-specific information.

\subsection{DeepTCR Architecture}

We used the official DeepTCR\_SS 2.1.29 large convolutional sequence classifier (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}).

\textbf{Sequence encoder and gene features.} The model combines convolutional features from the CDR3 sequence with the V and J features and classifies each sequence with a two-class output.

\textbf{Patient-level aggregation.} For held-out patient $i$ with $n_i$ eligible records, we averaged the responder-class score $s_{ij}$ across records:
\begin{equation}
\hat{s}_i = \frac{1}{n_i} \sum_{j=1}^{n_i} s_{ij}.
\end{equation}
This aggregation gives each stored sequence record the same weight. All models use the same patient scoring rule.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{architecture.png}
\caption{\textbf{DeepTCR Model Architecture.} The sequence classifier combines CDR3 and V/J inputs. The patient score is the mean of the sequence-level responder scores.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Training Configuration}

The repeated study used the following fixed settings:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Batch size:} 1,024 sequences.
    \item \textbf{Network size:} Large; sequence and gene inputs enabled.
    \item \textbf{Optimizer:} Adam with a learning rate of 0.001.
    \item \textbf{Epochs:} Ten per fit with all eligible records from the training patients. There was no early stopping or tuning on held-out outcomes.
    \item \textbf{Dropout rate:} 0.0.
\end{itemize}
Training used Python 3.9.18 and TensorFlow 2.12.0 on Apple Silicon CPU. The baseline used scikit-learn 1.2.2.

\subsection{Patient-Held-Out Cross-Validation}

We crossed five deterministic, stratified five-fold patient partitions (partition seeds 20261121--20261125) with two model and training-order seeds (20261201 and 20261202). The two seeds used identical patient assignments within each partition. The design produced ten complete five-fold evaluations and 50 fits.

Each fold was fit on patients outside its test set. Every patient received one held-out score per complete evaluation. As a sensitivity check, three- and four-fold evaluations reused the first partition seed and first training seed. This added seven fits.

\textbf{Matched composition baseline.} For each patient, 422 features summarized the mean and standard deviation of CDR3 length, the frequencies of 20 amino acids, and the frequencies of 400 adjacent amino acid pairs. Each scaler and L2-regularized logistic regression (\texttt{liblinear}, $C=0.1$) was fit on training patients from the same partition as DeepTCR.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

The primary AUROC gives equal weight to each responder--non-responder pair scored by the same fitted model. A pair receives full credit when the responder is ranked higher and half credit for a tie. We sum these credits over folds and divide by the total number of comparable pairs. Medians and ranges describe sensitivity across repeated evaluations of the same 34 patients. The shared cohort means these evaluations do not provide independent replicates for a confidence interval.

Pooled out-of-fold AUROC and average precision are reported as secondary descriptions. Pooled AUROC can compare scores produced by different fitted models. The composition baseline uses the same held-out comparisons as DeepTCR.

Two earlier complete five-fold runs used a common patient partition. Their 95\% intervals used 2,000 patient bootstrap draws stratified by test fold and outcome class, conditional on the fitted scores. The training-label control for fold 1 permuted the labels of its training patients and scored the same seven test patients. This control examines one fixed split.

\subsection{Unsupervised Learning: PCA and K-Means}

The original repertoire analysis was motivated by the unsupervised visualization in Sidhom et al. \citep{sidhom2021deeptcr}. It used PCA for feature reduction.

\textbf{Feature Extraction:} In the 30,000-record analysis, normalized CDR3 3-mer counts and one-hot V and J annotations were standardized before PCA. The archived result uses 128 principal components and ten K-means clusters.

\textbf{Two-dimensional Visualization:} The UMAP visualization used 10,000 records sampled from the PCA representation. The projection was fit without response labels. Recorded response was used to annotate the plots after fitting.

\textbf{Cluster Occupancy:} For each of ten clusters we reaggregated the archived within-patient percentages, with equal weight per patient within each response group. The separate 50,000-record descriptive analysis used 50 principal components, five K-means clusters, and a t-SNE view. Both archived runs sampled from the 34-patient cohort for visualization and exploratory analysis. The supervised evaluation used the full set of eligible records.

\subsection{Cohort Characteristics}

The study cohort comprised 34 patients with refractory/relapsed B-cell lymphoma treated with CD19 CAR-T cell therapy, with 18 responders and 16 non-responders. The archived input contained 239,637 TRB sequence records across all patients (Figure~\ref{fig:cohort}).

Its median advantage was 0.053 (range $-0.070$ to 0.175). Changing the training seed on a fixed partition moved AUROC by a median of 0.088 (range 0.070--0.140).

\subsection{Sequence-Level Interpretation}

The model assigns a responder-class score to each sequence record for patient-level averaging. The archived array previously used to rank sequences matches a seeded random fallback. This analysis therefore provides no validated ranking of individual sequences or evidence that any sequence is tumor-reactive.

\subsection{Sequence Characteristics}

\section{Discussion}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Principal Findings}

\textbf{Supervised Learning:} Across patient-separated five-fold evaluations, DeepTCR had median AUROC 0.456 and varied from 0.298 to 0.579. The model exceeded the matched composition baseline in most paired evaluations, but both medians were below 0.50. The three- and four-fold checks differed substantially. The available results do not establish reliable discrimination in unseen patients.

\textbf{Unsupervised Learning:} The original PCA and K-means analyses describe how sampled repertoires are distributed across clusters. The two response groups occupy overlapping regions. Cluster occupancy differences do not provide an independent check of prediction performance because they were estimated within the same cohort.

The earlier archived analysis reported 0.754 by summarizing the first 100 logged test-epoch scores. These were neither 100 independent fits nor patient-exclusive folds. Replaying the archived row split placed patients in both training and test. The present estimates use the patient as the unit of validation.

\subsection{Comparison with Existing CAR-T Biomarkers}

Current predictive biomarkers for CAR-T response include tumor burden, inflammatory markers (LDH, ferritin, CRP), and product characteristics such as CD4/CD8 ratio and exhaustion phenotypes \citep{locke2020tumor,deng2020characteristics}. Fraietta et al. demonstrated that CAR-T products with memory phenotypes and lower exhaustion markers show improved persistence and efficacy \citep{fraietta2018determinants}.

TCR repertoire analysis measures a different feature of the infused product. While existing biomarkers focus on surface phenotypes and metabolic states, TCR repertoire analysis captures the clonal composition and antigen-recognition potential of the T-cell pool. Whether these features add predictive value to existing biomarkers remains untested in this cohort.

\subsection{Biological Implications}

\textbf{T-cell fitness:} The endogenous TCR repertoire reflects the clonal history and differentiation state of T cells, which can influence CAR-T product quality \citep{fraietta2018determinants}. This study did not establish a relationship between specific sequences and cellular fitness.

\textbf{Gene usage:} The observed gene usage summaries reflect the repertoire composition of this cohort. They do not establish convergent selection or shared antigen specificity.

\textbf{Product heterogeneity:} The exploratory analyses show variation across the sequenced products. The biological meaning of particular clusters requires independent evidence.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the sample size of 34 patients, limits statistical precision and generalizability. There was no independent validation cohort.

Second, the cohort design (18 responders, 16 non-responders) may not reflect real-world CAR-T response rates, which vary by disease subtype and patient characteristics. Model calibration should be evaluated in populations with varying class distributions.

Third, clustering depends on the selected sequence representation, sampled records, and number of clusters. We did not measure the reproducibility of cluster occupancy on independent products.

Fourth, the repeated evaluations share the same patients. Their spread describes sensitivity to splits and training seeds. The earlier bootstrap intervals condition on fixed model scores and do not include variability from retraining. Evaluating transport to a later cohort would require an independent test.

\subsection{Future Directions}

Several extensions of this work are warranted:

\textbf{Multi-center validation:} Studies with larger sample sizes would establish generalizability across patient populations, CAR-T products, and sequencing platforms.

\textbf{Biomarker integration:} Future studies could test if TCR features improve prediction over a model with tumor burden and established product markers.

\textbf{Longitudinal analysis:} Tracking TCR repertoire evolution during CAR-T expansion and persistence could enable dynamic response prediction.

\textbf{Functional validation:} Future candidate sequences would need independent evidence of association before functional testing could establish a role in CAR-T efficacy.

\textbf{Manufacturing optimization:} If specific TCR subpopulations correlate with response, this could inform CAR-T manufacturing protocols to enrich for favorable T-cell populations.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%==============================================================================

This study evaluated DeepTCR response prediction from CD19 CAR-T product repertoires of 34 patients with refractory/relapsed B-cell lymphoma. Patient-held-out validation across 57 fits gave variable estimates across patient partitions and training seeds. The median five-fold AUROC was 0.456 and the matched composition baseline median was 0.386.

The exploratory repertoire analysis summarizes V/J usage, CDR3 length and composition, and PCA-based cluster occupancy in the observed cohort. The study did not establish a predictive repertoire signature. Larger independent cohorts with a predefined endpoint would be needed to assess whether repertoire features add value to established clinical and product measurements.
