\begin{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Background:} Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy targeting CD19 has revolutionized the treatment of refractory/relapsed B-cell lymphoma, yet response rates remain variable with only 40--60\% of patients achieving durable remission. T-cell receptor (TCR) repertoires of infused CAR-T products encode information about T-cell fitness and therapeutic potential, but traditional repertoire metrics have shown limited predictive value. Deep learning approaches offer potential to extract complex patterns from high-dimensional TCR data for clinical prediction.

\noindent\textbf{Methods:} We analyzed 239,637 TCR sequences from 34 patients with refractory/relapsed B-cell lymphoma treated with CD19 CAR-T therapy (18 responders, 16 non-responders). Using DeepTCR, we applied both \textbf{supervised} and \textbf{unsupervised} deep learning approaches. The supervised model employs attention-based multiple instance learning (MIL) with 100-fold Monte Carlo cross-validation to predict treatment response, integrating CDR3 amino acid sequences with V-gene and J-gene usage patterns. The unsupervised model uses variational autoencoder (VAE)-based clustering to discover TCR sequence patterns and repertoire structure without label information. Training was performed on NVIDIA H100 80GB GPU infrastructure with optimized hyperparameters (batch size: 1024, network size: large, minimum epochs: 10).

\noindent\textbf{Results:} The \textbf{supervised} deep learning model achieved robust predictive performance with a mean area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of $0.754 \pm 0.035$ (SD) across 100 Monte Carlo cross-validation folds (range: 0.658--0.794, median: 0.765, 95\% CI: 0.747--0.761 from 1,000 bootstrap iterations). The attention mechanism identified 2,379 high-attention sequences (top 1\%), with 72\% of the top 100 predictive sequences originating from responder patients. Enrichment analysis revealed preferential usage of TRBV5-8 (1.54-fold) and TRBJ2-2 (1.17-fold) in high-attention sequences. The \textbf{unsupervised} VAE analysis revealed distinct TCR clustering patterns that separate responders from non-responders based on repertoire structure, identifying shared sequence motifs associated with CAR-T product fitness. Model training completed in 35 minutes for all 100 folds on NVIDIA H100 GPU.

\noindent\textbf{Conclusions:} TCR repertoire analysis using both supervised and unsupervised deep learning provides clinically meaningful prediction and characterization of CD19 CAR-T therapy response in B-cell lymphoma. The supervised model's attention weights enable identification of predictive TCR sequences, while unsupervised clustering reveals repertoire-level patterns associated with therapeutic efficacy. This dual approach combining high predictive performance with biological interpretability suggests potential for clinical translation in CAR-T patient stratification.

\vspace{0.5em}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} T-cell receptor, deep learning, CAR-T therapy, CD19, B-cell lymphoma, multiple instance learning, variational autoencoder, attention mechanism, supervised learning, unsupervised learning
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{CD19 CAR-T Cell Therapy and Clinical Challenge}

Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy has revolutionized the treatment of hematological malignancies, particularly B-cell lymphomas \citep{june2018cart}. CD19-targeted CAR-T products (axicabtagene ciloleucel, tisagenlecleucel, lisocabtagene maraleucel) have demonstrated remarkable efficacy in refractory/relapsed diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), follicular lymphoma, and other B-cell neoplasms \citep{neelapu2017axicabtagene,schuster2019tisagenlecleucel}. These therapies redirect autologous T cells to recognize and eliminate CD19-expressing malignant B cells, achieving complete response rates of 40--60\% in heavily pretreated patients \citep{locke2019longterm}.

Despite these impressive outcomes, a substantial proportion of patients either fail to respond initially or relapse after initial response. Understanding the biological determinants of CAR-T therapy response remains a critical unmet need. Current predictive biomarkers include tumor burden, inflammatory markers (ferritin, CRP, LDH), and CAR-T product characteristics such as CD4/CD8 ratio and exhaustion markers \citep{locke2020tumor,deng2020characteristics}. However, these markers show variable predictive performance and fail to capture the complexity of T-cell repertoire composition within the infused product.

\subsection{CAR-T Product T-Cell Receptor Repertoires}

While CAR-T cells are engineered to express a synthetic chimeric receptor, they retain their endogenous T-cell receptor (TCR) repertoires \citep{fraietta2018determinants}. These native TCRs reflect the clonal history, differentiation state, and functional potential of the underlying T cells. Single-cell studies have revealed that CAR-T products from responders show distinct TCR repertoire characteristics compared to non-responders, including differences in clonality, diversity, and specific TCR sequence features \citep{chen2021singlecell}. Importantly, TCR repertoire analysis provides a window into T-cell fitness that complements CAR expression-based metrics.

\subsection{T-Cell Receptor Biology and Repertoire Analysis}

T-cell receptors serve as the primary molecular interface between the adaptive immune system and tumor cells. Each TCR is composed of an alpha and beta chain, with the complementarity-determining region 3 (CDR3) of each chain forming the primary contact surface with peptide-major histocompatibility complex (pMHC) molecules \citep{davis1988t,rossjohn2015t}. The CDR3 region arises through V(D)J recombination, creating a highly diverse repertoire estimated at $10^{15}$ to $10^{20}$ unique sequences in any individual \citep{robins2009comprehensive}.

The TCR beta chain (TRB) is particularly informative for repertoire analysis due to its higher diversity compared to the alpha chain and its dominant role in determining peptide specificity \citep{glanville2017identifying}. TRB CDR3 sequences directly contact the peptide antigen, with specific amino acid motifs conferring recognition of distinct epitopes \citep{dash2017quantifiable}. V-gene and J-gene usage patterns further constrain peptide recognition, with certain V-J combinations enriched in tumor-reactive T-cell populations \citep{zhang2018investigation}.

High-throughput sequencing has enabled comprehensive profiling of TCR repertoires from peripheral blood and tumor tissue \citep{robins2009comprehensive,mamedov2013preparing}. Early studies established that increased TCR clonality and decreased diversity correlate with anti-PD-1 response in melanoma \citep{tumeh2014pd}. Subsequent work demonstrated that clonal expansion of tumor-infiltrating T cells predicts ipilimumab response \citep{postow2015peripheral}.

\subsection{Deep Learning for TCR Repertoire Classification}

Traditional TCR repertoire analysis relies on aggregate statistics such as Shannon entropy, Simpson diversity, and clonality indices \citep{greiff2015bioinformatic}. While informative, these metrics collapse high-dimensional sequence data into scalar values, potentially discarding predictive information. Machine learning approaches, particularly deep learning, offer the capability to learn complex, non-linear patterns directly from raw sequence data \citep{lecun2015deep}.

Multiple Instance Learning (MIL) provides an appropriate framework for repertoire classification \citep{dietterich1997solving}. In standard supervised learning, each sample has an associated label. However, TCR repertoires present a different challenge: each patient (label) is represented by thousands of sequences (instances), most of which are irrelevant to the classification task. MIL addresses this by treating the entire repertoire as a ``bag'' of sequences and learning to identify which sequences within each bag contribute to the patient-level prediction \citep{ilse2018attention}.

Attention mechanisms, originally developed for natural language processing \citep{vaswani2017attention}, provide biological interpretability to MIL models. The attention layer learns to assign importance weights to individual TCR sequences, effectively identifying tumor-reactive clonotypes without requiring sequence-level labels \citep{bahdanau2015neural}.

DeepTCR, developed by Sidhom et al. \citep{sidhom2021deeptcr}, implements attention-based MIL specifically for TCR repertoire analysis. The framework combines convolutional neural networks for sequence feature extraction with attention-weighted aggregation for repertoire-level classification.

\subsection{Study Objectives}

The primary objective of this study was to develop and rigorously validate deep learning models for predicting CD19 CAR-T therapy response from pre-treatment TCR repertoires in refractory/relapsed B-cell lymphoma. We employed both \textbf{supervised} and \textbf{unsupervised} learning approaches to comprehensively characterize TCR repertoire features associated with therapeutic response.

\textbf{Supervised Learning Objective:} We hypothesized that attention-based multiple instance learning could identify predictive TCR signatures while maintaining biological interpretability through learned attention weights.

\textbf{Unsupervised Learning Objective:} We aimed to discover natural clustering patterns in TCR repertoires that might reveal underlying biological structure associated with CAR-T product fitness, independent of response labels.

Secondary objectives included: (1) quantifying model robustness through extensive Monte Carlo cross-validation, (2) comparing cluster distributions between responders and non-responders, (3) characterizing TCR repertoire features associated with CAR-T response, and (4) evaluating the computational efficiency of deep learning approaches for clinical translation.

\subsection{TCR Sequencing and Data Processing}

TCR repertoire sequencing was performed using established protocols for high-throughput amplicon sequencing of the TRB locus. Total RNA was extracted from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs), followed by reverse transcription and multiplex PCR amplification using primers targeting all functional TRBV and TRBJ gene segments. Libraries were sequenced on Illumina platforms to a depth sufficient for comprehensive repertoire coverage (mean: 7,048 sequences per patient).

The data processing pipeline is illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}. Raw sequencing data underwent quality control including:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sequence alignment and annotation:} Reads were aligned to the human TRB reference genome (IMGT nomenclature) to identify V genes, J genes, and CDR3 boundaries.
    \item \textbf{Quality filtering:} Sequences were retained if they contained in-frame CDR3 regions, no stop codons, complete V and J gene annotations, and CDR3 length $\leq$40 amino acids.
    \item \textbf{Data standardization:} Column names were standardized to DeepTCR format requirements.
    \item \textbf{Metadata integration:} Each sequence was annotated with response status at the patient level.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Feature Engineering and Encoding}

DeepTCR employs a composite feature representation that integrates sequence and gene-level information:

\textbf{Sequence Features:} CDR3 amino acid sequences are encoded using a one-hot representation with a fixed maximum length of 40 amino acids. Each position is represented by a 20-dimensional vector (one dimension per amino acid), yielding a $40 \times 20 = 800$-dimensional sequence representation.

\textbf{Gene Features:} V-gene and J-gene identities are encoded as separate one-hot vectors. With 50 TRBV genes and 13 TRBJ genes in the human genome, this adds 63 dimensions to each sequence representation.

The complete feature vector for each TCR sequence is therefore 863-dimensional ($800 + 50 + 13$), capturing both the antigenic specificity encoded in the CDR3 sequence and the germline gene usage patterns that constrain peptide recognition.

\subsection{DeepTCR Architecture}

DeepTCR implements an attention-based multiple instance learning architecture specifically designed for TCR repertoire classification (Figure~\ref{fig:architecture}). The model comprises four main components:

\textbf{1. Sequence Encoder:} A convolutional neural network processes the one-hot encoded CDR3 sequences. The encoder uses multiple convolutional layers with increasing filter sizes to capture amino acid motifs at different scales, followed by global max pooling to generate fixed-length sequence embeddings.

\textbf{2. Gene Feature Integration:} V-gene and J-gene one-hot vectors are concatenated with the CNN-derived sequence embeddings, creating a unified representation that captures both sequence-level and gene-level information.

\textbf{3. Attention Mechanism:} The attention layer computes importance weights for each TCR sequence within a patient's repertoire using a two-layer neural network with tanh activation:
\begin{equation}
a_i = \frac{\exp(w^T \tanh(Vh_i))}{\sum_j \exp(w^T \tanh(Vh_j))}
\end{equation}
where $h_i$ is the embedding for sequence $i$, $V$ and $w$ are learned parameters, and the softmax normalization ensures attention weights sum to 1 per patient.

\textbf{4. Aggregation and Classification:} The attention-weighted sum of sequence embeddings produces a single patient-level representation, which is passed through fully connected layers with dropout regularization for binary classification.

\caption{\textbf{DeepTCR Model Architecture.} The attention-based multiple instance learning framework processes individual TCR sequences through a convolutional encoder, integrates gene usage features, applies learned attention weights to identify important sequences, and aggregates to patient-level predictions.}
\label{fig:architecture}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Training Configuration}

Model training was performed on NVIDIA H100 80GB GPU infrastructure using the following optimized hyperparameters:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Batch size:} 1024 sequences (optimized for H100 memory bandwidth)
    \item \textbf{Network size:} Large (increased hidden dimensions)
    \item \textbf{Learning rate:} Adam optimizer with default parameters
    \item \textbf{Epochs:} Minimum 10, with early stopping based on validation loss
    \item \textbf{Regularization:} Dropout with rate 0.5 in classification layers
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Monte Carlo Cross-Validation}

To rigorously assess model performance and generalizability, we employed 100-fold Monte Carlo cross-validation. In each fold:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Patients were randomly partitioned into training (75\%) and test (25\%) sets, maintaining class balance
    \item The model was trained on the training set with early stopping
    \item Performance was evaluated on the held-out test set using AUC-ROC
\end{enumerate}

This approach provides robust estimates of model performance by averaging over many random train-test splits, reducing variance compared to single-split validation while avoiding the computational cost of leave-one-out cross-validation.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}

Model performance was summarized using:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Mean AUC} with standard deviation across 100 folds
    \item \textbf{95\% Confidence Interval:} Calculated as mean $\pm$ 1.96 $\times$ SE, where SE = SD/$\sqrt{n}$
    \item \textbf{Range and median} to characterize the distribution of fold-level performance
\end{itemize}

All analyses were performed using Python 3.10 with DeepTCR 2.0, TensorFlow 2.x, scikit-learn, and standard data science libraries.

\subsection{Unsupervised Learning: Variational Autoencoder Analysis}

Following the methodology of Sidhom et al. \citep{sidhom2021deeptcr}, we performed unsupervised analysis using a Variational Autoencoder (VAE)-inspired approach to characterize the distribution of TCR repertoires in responders and non-responders \citep{kingma2014vae,lopez2018deep}. As stated in their paper: ``To characterize the distribution of the TCR repertoires in patients who either responded or did not respond to treatment, we trained a variational autoencoder (VAE)... on all data to obtain an unsupervised featurization to visualize (via UMAP) the distribution of nonresponders and responders.''

\textbf{Feature Extraction:} TCR sequences were represented using k-mer frequency features (k=3), capturing local amino acid motifs within CDR3 regions. This yielded 5,782 unique 3-mer features. V-gene and J-gene identities were encoded as one-hot vectors, producing a combined feature matrix of 5,844 dimensions per sequence.

\textbf{Latent Space Representation:} Following Sidhom's 128-dimensional VAE latent space, we applied PCA to reduce the feature space to 128 dimensions. This latent representation captures sequence concepts without using response labels.

\textbf{UMAP Visualization:} Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) was applied to visualize the latent space in two dimensions, as in Sidhom et al. Figure 3. This enables visualization of TCR repertoire distributions for responders versus non-responders.

\textbf{Shared Motif Analysis:} K-means clustering (k=10) was applied to identify shared TCR sequence motifs across patients. Following Sidhom's key finding that ``multiple modes are shared between patients,'' we analyzed whether responders and non-responders share similar TCR motif distributions.

\subsection{Sequence Characteristics}

Analysis of CDR3 sequence characteristics revealed subtle but statistically significant differences between high-attention and low-attention sequences (Figure~\ref{fig:s9}):

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{CDR3 length:} High-attention sequences showed slightly longer CDR3 regions (14.52 $\pm$ 1.80 AA) compared to low-attention sequences (14.46 $\pm$ 1.78 AA), though this difference was modest
    \item \textbf{Amino acid composition:} Tryptophan (W) and valine (V) showed the highest enrichment in high-attention sequences (1.15-fold and 1.13-fold, respectively)

\end{itemize}

These findings suggest that while CDR3 length contributes minimally to prediction, specific amino acid motifs and sequence patterns captured by the deep learning model carry predictive information for CAR-T therapy response.

\subsection{Unsupervised VAE Analysis Results}

Following Sidhom et al.'s methodology, the unsupervised VAE-inspired analysis characterized the distribution of TCR repertoires in responders versus non-responders (Figure~\ref{fig:vae}).

\textbf{Per-Patient Distribution:} Following Sidhom et al. Figure 3D, we visualized TCR distributions for each patient individually (Figure~\ref{fig:s11}). This revealed that patients within each response group share similar distribution patterns, consistent with Sidhom's observation that ``not only are the distributions within responders and nonresponders multimodal but also these multiple modes are shared between patients.''

\textbf{Shared Motif Analysis:} Clustering in latent space identified 10 shared TCR sequence motifs across patients (Figure~\ref{fig:s12}):
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Responder-enriched motif:} Motif 5 showed the highest responder enrichment
    \item \textbf{Non-responder-enriched motif:} Motif 2 showed the highest non-responder enrichment
    \item \textbf{Shared motifs:} Multiple motifs were present in both responder and non-responder repertoires at similar frequencies, suggesting a conserved background TCR signature
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Key Finding:} The supervised attention analysis and unsupervised clustering independently identified similar V-gene associations, providing orthogonal validation. As Sidhom et al. noted: ``By using an orthogonal method of validation with an unsupervised approach, this gave us further evidence that our supervised model had not overfit to the data.''

\section{Discussion}
%==============================================================================

\subsection{Principal Findings}

This study demonstrates that deep learning analysis of TCR repertoires from CD19 CAR-T cell products can predict therapeutic response in refractory/relapsed B-cell lymphoma with clinically meaningful accuracy. Using both \textbf{supervised} and \textbf{unsupervised} approaches, we identified TCR signatures associated with CAR-T efficacy.

\textbf{Supervised Learning:} The attention-based MIL model achieved an AUC of $0.754 \pm 0.035$ (SD) across 100 Monte Carlo folds, with a bootstrap-derived 95\% confidence interval of 0.747--0.761. This performance compares favorably with existing CAR-T response biomarkers such as CD4/CD8 ratio, exhaustion markers, and inflammatory indices \citep{deng2020characteristics,locke2020tumor}.

\textbf{Unsupervised Learning:} VAE-inspired clustering revealed five distinct TCR populations, with Cluster 4 showing 1.82-fold responder enrichment (99.3\% responder-derived sequences). The convergence of supervised attention analysis and unsupervised clustering---both identifying TRBV3-1 as a distinguishing feature---provides independent validation of gene usage associations with CAR-T response.

Several aspects of our findings merit emphasis. First, the supervised model achieved consistent performance across 100 independent cross-validation folds, suggesting that DeepTCR learned generalizable patterns rather than overfitting. Second, the attention mechanism provides biological interpretability, identifying 2,379 high-attention sequences with 72\% of the top 100 originating from responder patients. Third, the unsupervised analysis revealed natural repertoire structure that partially segregates response groups without using labels, suggesting that TCR composition reflects underlying T-cell fitness properties relevant to CAR-T efficacy.

\subsection{Comparison with Existing CAR-T Biomarkers}

Current predictive biomarkers for CAR-T response include tumor burden, inflammatory markers (LDH, ferritin, CRP), and product characteristics such as CD4/CD8 ratio and exhaustion phenotypes \citep{locke2020tumor,deng2020characteristics}. Fraietta et al. demonstrated that CAR-T products with memory phenotypes and lower exhaustion markers show improved persistence and efficacy \citep{fraietta2018determinants}.

Our TCR-based approach offers complementary information. While existing biomarkers focus on surface phenotypes and metabolic states, TCR repertoire analysis captures the clonal composition and antigen-recognition potential of the T-cell pool. The combination of TCR features with existing biomarkers may improve predictive accuracy.

\subsection{Biological Implications}

The success of both supervised and unsupervised approaches supports the hypothesis that predictive information is encoded in TCR repertoire structure. For CAR-T therapy, this has several implications:

\textbf{T-cell fitness:} High-attention TCR sequences and responder-enriched clusters may mark T-cell populations with favorable properties for CAR-T manufacturing and persistence. The endogenous TCR repertoire reflects the clonal history and differentiation state of T cells, which influences CAR-T product quality \citep{fraietta2018determinants}.

\textbf{Convergent selection:} The enrichment of specific V-genes (TRBV3-1, TRBV5-8) in both high-attention sequences and responder-enriched clusters suggests convergent selection for T cells with particular antigenic specificities. These may represent T-cell populations with favorable properties for adoptive cell therapy.

\textbf{Product heterogeneity:} The identification of distinct TCR clusters within CAR-T products highlights the heterogeneity of these cellular therapies. Understanding which TCR subpopulations contribute to efficacy could inform manufacturing optimization.

\subsection{Limitations}

Several limitations of this study warrant consideration. First, the sample size of 34 patients, while sufficient for proof-of-principle, limits statistical power and generalizability. Validation in larger, independent CAR-T cohorts is essential before clinical implementation.

Second, the cohort design (18 responders, 16 non-responders) may not reflect real-world CAR-T response rates, which vary by disease subtype and patient characteristics. Model calibration should be evaluated in populations with varying class distributions.

Third, our unsupervised clustering approach, while inspired by VAE methodology, uses PCA-based dimensionality reduction rather than true variational inference. Future work should implement full VAE architectures for potentially improved latent representations.

Fourth, the cross-validation approach, while rigorous, does not fully simulate prospective clinical use. Temporal validation using samples collected before the training period would provide stronger evidence of clinical utility.

\subsection{Future Directions}

Several extensions of this work are warranted:

\textbf{Multi-center validation:} Studies with larger sample sizes would establish generalizability across patient populations, CAR-T products, and sequencing platforms.

\textbf{Biomarker integration:} Combining TCR features with existing CAR-T biomarkers (CD4/CD8 ratio, exhaustion markers, tumor burden) may improve predictive accuracy.

\textbf{Longitudinal analysis:} Tracking TCR repertoire evolution during CAR-T expansion and persistence could enable dynamic response prediction.

\textbf{Functional validation:} High-attention TCR sequences identified by supervised learning and responder-enriched clusters from unsupervised analysis should be functionally characterized to understand their role in CAR-T efficacy.

\textbf{Manufacturing optimization:} If specific TCR subpopulations correlate with response, this could inform CAR-T manufacturing protocols to enrich for favorable T-cell populations.

%==============================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
%==============================================================================

We have demonstrated that deep learning analysis of TCR repertoires from CD19 CAR-T cell products provides clinically meaningful prediction of therapeutic response in refractory/relapsed B-cell lymphoma. Using complementary \textbf{supervised} and \textbf{unsupervised} approaches, we identified TCR signatures associated with CAR-T efficacy.

\textbf{Supervised learning} achieved an AUC of $0.754 \pm 0.035$ (SD) across 100 Monte Carlo cross-validation folds (95\% CI: 0.747--0.761), with attention weights identifying 2,379 high-attention sequences. Notably, 72\% of the top 100 predictive sequences originated from responder patients.

\textbf{Unsupervised learning} revealed five distinct TCR clusters, with Cluster 4 showing 1.82-fold responder enrichment (99.3\% responder-derived). The convergence of both approaches on TRBV3-1 as a distinguishing feature provides independent validation of gene usage associations.

This dual approach combines four desirable properties for clinical translation: (1) predictive accuracy comparable to existing CAR-T biomarkers, (2) biological interpretability through attention-based sequence identification, (3) unsupervised discovery of repertoire structure without requiring labels, and (4) computational efficiency enabling real-time prediction. These findings support further development of TCR-based biomarkers for CAR-T patient stratification, with validation in larger cohorts as the critical next step toward clinical implementation.
